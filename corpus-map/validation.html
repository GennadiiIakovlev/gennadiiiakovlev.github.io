Does speech negativity track what voters feel?

Convergent validity of the parliamentary out-party measure against Orhan's (2022) survey-based Affective Polarization Index (API), built from CSES like-dislike scores across 52 democracies and 178 elections. Each survey observation is paired with the parliamentary speech of the legislature that preceded that election.

The Westminster offset

Parliaments in the Westminster tradition talk about the other side roughly twice as harshly as the rest of the sample while their voters report slightly milder polarization. All five sit below the fitted line, and a Westminster dummy absorbs about one full survey point. Question Period and Prime Minister's Questions are adversarial by design: the opposition's institutional role in the chamber is to attack the government, so spoken hostility runs ahead of how citizens feel about each other's parties. Consensus systems produce the opposite gap (Chile, the low outlier, is the clearest case). We read this as a real institutional difference the speech measure registers faithfully, not as measurement error, and report the pooled and ex-Westminster figures side by side.

Robustness to the pairing window

The preceding-legislature rule is the paper's specification. Alternatives that centre a symmetric window on the election year, or include the election year itself, correlate less well on both this and earlier builds of the panel: post-election speech cannot have shaped pre-election attitudes, and it dilutes the signal.
