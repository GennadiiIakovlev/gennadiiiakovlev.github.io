Does speech negativity track what voters feel?

Convergent validity of the parliamentary out-party measure against Orhan's (2022) survey-based Affective Polarization Index (API), built from CSES like-dislike scores across 52 democracies and 178 elections. Each survey observation is paired with the parliamentary speech of the legislature that preceded that election.

Election-level observations and country means

x: out-party negativity in parliamentary speech, the mention-weighted mean of the negated out-party evaluation over the legislature preceding the election (previous election + 1 to election year − 1; five-year fallback when the previous election is more than six years back; cells with at least 50 out-party mentions). y: Orhan's API for that election. Dashed line: least-squares fit. Hover for the country and year.

Westminster chambers

In this build, parliaments in the Westminster tradition (AUS CAN GBR NZL USA) talk about the other side only slightly more harshly than the rest of the sample (1.1×), while their voters report slightly milder polarization (survey index 4.30 against 4.55). 3 of the 5 sit below the fitted line, and a Westminster dummy shifts the survey index by -0.36 points. Builds before 2026-09-27 showed a much larger gap (about 1.8×, and a dummy near −1 point); most of it disappeared when the aggregator stopped merging distinct mentions that shared an ID, a bug that had hit the British (68% of mentions merged) and Australian (60%) corpora hardest. Chile has the lowest speech negativity in the sample, with its survey index above the line. The toggle above shows the pooled and ex-Westminster figures side by side.

Robustness to the pairing window

The preceding-legislature rule is the paper's specification: post-election speech cannot have shaped pre-election attitudes. Adding the election year itself changes the fit by +0.003 at the election level and -0.025 on country means in this build; the full grid is below.
